In nineteen nineteen, a wave of syrup twenty-five feet tall tore through the streets of Boston.
It was January fifteenth, and the North End was busy at lunchtime.
By the harbor stood a giant tank, five stories high, holding two point three million gallons of molasses.
A rum company used it to make alcohol, racing to beat Prohibition.
The tank had leaked for years. The company just painted it brown so nobody would notice.
Then, just after noon, the rivets popped like gunshots.
The tank split open, and a wall of molasses rushed out at thirty-five miles an hour.
It knocked a firehouse off its foundation and bent the steel of the elevated railway.
Twenty-one people died, and a hundred and fifty were hurt.
Because it was so cold, the molasses set like glue, and rescuers could barely move.
Sailors from a navy training ship waded in to pull people out.
The harbor stayed brown until summer.
And locals say that on hot days, the North End still smells sweet.
