In 1938, a pilot took off for California and landed in Ireland.
Douglas Corrigan dreamed of flying across the Atlantic.
He'd even helped build Lindbergh's famous plane, the Spirit of St. Louis.
But officials ruled his nine-year-old plane too unsafe to try.
He'd paid about three hundred dollars for it.
So he filed a flight plan home to California instead.
At dawn, he took off from New York, then turned east.
No radio. His food: two chocolate bars and two boxes of fig bars.
Then it got worse: gasoline started leaking into the cockpit.
So he punched a hole in the floor with a screwdriver to drain it.
For twenty-eight hours, he saw nothing but clouds and ocean.
He later claimed he only looked down after twenty-six hours.
Then he landed in Dublin and said: "Where am I? I intended to fly to California."
His excuse? Fog, and he followed the wrong end of his compass needle.
Nobody believed him. But America loved it.
New York threw him a ticker-tape parade bigger than Lindbergh's.
His punishment: a fourteen-day suspension. Exactly as long as his boat ride home.
Until he died, he swore he was flying to California.
