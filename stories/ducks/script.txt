In 1992, 28,800 bath toys fell into the middle of the Pacific.
A storm hit a cargo ship sailing from Hong Kong to America.
Twelve containers went overboard. One was full of plastic ducks, frogs, beavers and turtles.
The boxes dissolved, and the toys popped free.
So a yellow army of ducks set sail, with no captain and no map.
Ten months later, ten of them washed up in Alaska, 2,000 miles away.
Then oceanographer Curtis Ebbesmeyer saw a free experiment on ocean currents.
So he asked beachcombers to report every duck they found.
Hundreds turned up along the coast of Alaska.
But that wasn't even the strangest part.
Some drifted north, through the Bering Strait, into the Arctic.
They froze into the ice and drifted for years, toward the Atlantic.
The sun bleached the ducks white, but the frogs stayed green.
By 2003, the toy company offered a reward for any found in New England.
Today collectors pay hundreds for one faded duck.
So how does a bath duck end up crossing the Arctic?
